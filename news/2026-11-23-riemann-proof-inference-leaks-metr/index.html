AI digest: Claude-assisted Riemann proof, leaky inference resellers, METR warning

Nov 23, 2026

Lean checkers back a Claude-assisted Riemann proof, Dragos exposes leaky AI resellers, and METR says AI is outpacing safety tests.

Lean community backs a Claude-assisted Riemann hypothesis proof

On November 20, Anthropic researcher Levent Alpöge released a proof of the generalized Riemann hypothesis, the 1859 problem tied to a $1 million Clay prize. The proof was developed with help from Claude and written as roughly 17 million lines of Lean code. Two days later the Lean and Mathlib community said dozens of independent runs confirmed the code checks and that it is fully confident in the result; a human-readable version is promised in the coming months. Machine-checkable proofs are becoming a practical way to trust AI-assisted work.

Cheap AI inference resellers caught reading and stealing data

Security firm Dragos sent about two million test requests with fake credentials through AI inference providers between July and November 2026 and found at least three compromised services, none of which had noticed. In the worst case, hundreds of planted AWS keys were harvested, and zero-data-retention promises were often ignored, The Decoder reports. OpenRouter reacted by removing three providers and withdrawing zero-retention approval from 125 endpoints. Every middleman between an app and a model can see the prompts, so the cheapest route is not always the safest.

METR: AI capabilities are outpacing safety testing

In its fourth-quarter update, the evaluation group METR says the length of tasks frontier models can finish keeps doubling about every 3.6 months, and that models such as Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra beat experienced professionals on most well-defined coding tasks. According to The Decoder's summary, the models are still weaker on open-ended problems without quick feedback, and METR warns that easy-to-run benchmarks may overstate real-world performance. It adds that current evidence cannot rule out a sharp speed-up in AI research. For anyone deploying agents, benchmark scores are a starting point, not a guarantee.

What it means for business

First, treat the AI supply chain like any other vendor chain: know which provider actually processes your requests, keep secrets out of prompts and rotate keys that may have passed through third parties. Second, test agents on your own real workflows instead of trusting public leaderboards. Third, where accuracy matters, build in verification such as automated checks, tests or human review. Whether you work with VA Corporation or another team, start an agent project with a narrow, measurable task and expand once the results hold up.
