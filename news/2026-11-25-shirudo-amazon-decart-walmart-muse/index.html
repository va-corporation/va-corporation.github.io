AI digest: OpenAI and Cisco's Shirudo, Amazon buys Decart tech, Walmart opens to Muse

Nov 25, 2026

Open-source shields for unpatched Linux, a reported $7.5B bet on chip software, and AI agents shopping Black Friday deals at Walmart and Gopuff.

OpenAI and Cisco release Shirudo to shield unpatched Linux

OpenAI and Cisco have released Shirudo, an open-source runtime for Linux built on eBPF. The idea is to load small, cryptographically signed “shields” into a running system that block a known exploit while the real patch is still being rolled out, with no restart. According to OpenAI, a shield written by its Codex agent stopped a Java4Shell exploit against a live Elasticsearch service in a controlled test run by the developers themselves. Help Net Security notes that production use needs a patched Linux kernel and a TPM 2.0 chip, so this is an early project rather than a drop-in product.

Amazon buys Decart's chip-optimization tech and team

Amazon has agreed to acquire the technology behind Decart's optimization stack together with the Israeli startup's founders and researchers; Bloomberg puts the price at about $7.5 billion. The software helps AI models run efficiently on chips from Nvidia, Google and Amazon itself. The deal comes as Amazon prepares to sell its Trainium accelerators to outside customers. It shows that the AI hardware race is increasingly decided by software that squeezes more out of every chip.

Walmart and Gopuff let Meta's Muse agent shop for customers

Walmart has opened its Black Friday deals to Meta's Muse assistant through Walmart Agent Gateway, launched in October to let outside AI agents connect directly to its online store. Shoppers link their Walmart account and describe what they want, and the agent searches, compares and builds a cart; Walmart says users can set spending limits, require approval for purchases and review the agent's orders. On the same day, delivery service Gopuff launched its own Muse connector covering more than 5,000 products. Retailers now have to decide whether to block shopping agents or open a controlled door for them.

What it means for business

All three stories point to one shift: AI agents are moving from chat windows into systems that act — protecting servers, placing orders, running on cheaper hardware. For companies, the practical lesson is to design the guardrails first: signed and logged actions, clear permission and spending limits, and human approval for anything irreversible. It is also worth preparing your catalog, API or internal data so agents can use them in a structured way instead of scraping web pages.
